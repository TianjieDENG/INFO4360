Course approach

Teaching text analytics when the model can already do it

Large language models changed what a text-analytics course needs to be. This one is built around a single skill: choosing, building, and defending the right approach for the problem in front of you.

Why the course was rebuilt

Classical text-analytics curricula taught a fixed pipeline: clean the text, turn it into a term–document matrix, run a classical classifier, add topic models for structure and a sentiment lexicon for tone. Every step was a technique to be learned in isolation.

A capable language model now matches or beats much of that pipeline from a plain-language prompt, with no training data and no feature engineering. A course that simply tours the old techniques invites the question students will rightly ask on day one: why not just ask the model? The honest answer — sometimes you should, and sometimes doing so is slow, expensive, unaccountable, or wrong — is the real subject of the course.

The organizing idea: a ladder of approaches

For any text task there is a spectrum of ways to solve it, from the heaviest to the lightest:

  • zero-shot LLM
  • few-shot LLM
  • retrieval-augmented generation
  • embeddings + a small classifier
  • a fine-tuned small model
  • TF-IDF + logistic regression

Each rung trades cost, latency, interpretability, data requirements, and privacy differently. The course teaches students to move up and down this ladder deliberately — to reach for the lightweight option when it is good enough, and to recognize when only a language model will do. The classical methods stay in the curriculum, but as living baselines that a heavier approach has to beat, not as history.

Evaluation as the through-line

Language models are fluent and confident even when they are wrong, which makes evaluation more important, not less. Every unit returns to it: labeled test sets and honest error analysis, precision and recall rather than a single accuracy number, inter-annotator agreement, groundedness and hallucination checks for generated text, and the use — and the failure modes — of a model grading another model's output.

Teaching judgment, including “no”

A question recurs through the term: should this be a language model, a cheap classical model, or a few lines of deterministic code? Students practice declining the powerful, opaque option when a simpler one will do, and justifying that call to someone who has to act on the result. The final unit is explicitly about cost, risk, privacy, and deployment — the conditions under which reaching for an LLM is the wrong professional choice.

What students leave able to do

  • Frame a fuzzy business question as a concrete text task — classification, extraction, clustering, retrieval.
  • Prepare and explore real text data, and represent it as features a model can use.
  • Build and evaluate models across the ladder, and read the evaluation honestly.
  • Work with language models deliberately: prompting, structured extraction, retrieval-augmented generation, and output evaluation.
  • Choose an approach on a cost, accuracy, interpretability, and privacy frontier — and defend the choice with evidence.
  • Communicate a recommendation, and what it gets wrong, to a decision-maker.
